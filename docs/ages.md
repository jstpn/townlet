# Ages: a research reference

Purpose: a sourced map of the Old World arc from foraging camp to motor city, so the design discussion about Townlet's ages can argue from dated evidence rather than memory. It describes and compares; it recommends nothing. Dates are approximate and regional, the text says where sources disagree, and [n] cites the Sources list.

## 1. Palaeolithic and Mesolithic

- **Dates.** Lower Palaeolithic c. 3.3 Ma to 300 ka, Middle 300-50 ka, Upper 50-12 ka, hunter-gatherer throughout, with boundaries that differ by region [96]. The European Mesolithic (c. 15,000-5000 BP; "Epipalaeolithic" in the Near East) follows the last glacial maximum and is short or absent where farming came early [95]. The three-age scheme is a museum sorting system (Thomsen 1816-36, Lubbock 1865) that fits Europe, the Mediterranean and the Near East and little else [56].
- **Enabling chain.** Knapped flint (Oldowan 2.6 Ma, hand axes 1.76 Ma, prepared cores c. 200 ka) gives butchery, woodworking and the hafted spear [96]. Fire is claimed at 1.0-1.79 Ma but widely accepted only from c. 400-300 ka, with deliberate fire-making c. 400 ka; it gives cooking, warmth, heat-treated stone and a longer day [99]. From c. 50 ka: bone tools, the spear-thrower c. 30 ka, nets, bows (which need microliths), fishing, art, burial [96]. Post-glacial warming and microliths hafted with birch tar give a fishing-and-fowling economy [95]. The dog, from wolves scavenging camp waste, is the first domestic animal, undisputed by c. 17,500 BP [177].
- **Settlement.** Waterside camps of brush or mammoth-bone huts around a hearth, reoccupied year after year [96][95]. Pottery appears among foragers in China c. 20,000 BP and Jomon Japan, so it is not a farming marker [17].
- **Production chains.** Flint and wood to tools; game and fish to meat, hide and bone; fibre to cordage and nets; fire to cooked food [96][95]. Wild-cereal harvesting with grinding stones comes before farming: granaries at Dhra' (11,300 BP) stored wild barley a millennium before domestication [29][101].
- **Civic life and hazards.** Band and kin; ritual and burial from the Upper Palaeolithic [96]. Göbekli Tepe (c. 9500-8000 BC): part-settled foragers raised monumental enclosures beside houses and cisterns, so the communal monument precedes farming [175]. Hazards: seasonal hunger, cold, predators [99].
- **Population.** Bands of 20-100 [96].

## 2. Neolithic

- **Dates.** c. 10,000 BC in the Near East (Pre-Pottery Neolithic A and B) to c. 4500 BC there; south-east Europe by c. 6500 BC, north-west Europe c. 4500-1700 BC, China c. 9500-2000 BC; Australia had none. It overlaps other ages elsewhere by millennia [101].
- **Enabling chain.** Sedentism came first: Natufian villages depended on wild cereals, and at Gilgal I near Jericho, fig cultivation c. 9400 BC (11,400 BP) may have preceded grain by up to a millennium [178]. Founder crops (emmer, einkorn, barley, lentil, pea, chickpea, bitter vetch, flax) in the 9th millennium BC; goats, sheep, cattle and pigs at the same time or slightly later [4][121]. The "revolution" is now read as gradual, and the package arrived in different orders: the Fertile Crescent farmed for three millennia before its first pots (c. 6400 BC), while Jomon Japan potted without farming [2][4]. Polished stone axes clear forest; mud brick (Jericho c. 8300 BC) gives permanent houses; hemp cloth c. 7000 BC, with thread supply, not the loom, limiting output [101][129][130].
- **Settlement.** Catalhoyuk (7100-5700 BC): shared-wall mud-brick houses with no streets, entered by ladder through the roof, rebuilt on the same footprint about once a generation (20-45 years) [111], shrines and burials inside houses, no public buildings [165]. Jericho: round houses behind a 3.6 m wall and 8.5 m tower c. 8300 BC, defence or flood barrier disputed [129]. Skara Brae (c. 3180-2500 BC): stone houses with built-in furniture and per-house drains [27].
- **Production chains.** Grain to sickle, quern, granary and bread; herds to meat, milk, hides, wool and dung fuel; clay to pots; flax and wool to cloth; timber and flint to axes and houses [101][4][130].
- **Civic life and hazards.** Household shrine, shared granary, communal monument; no ruler visible [175][165]. Farming raised fertility (shorter birth spacing on a starchy diet), the Neolithic demographic transition, and brought crowding disease with it [108]. Crop failure and flood; storage is the mitigation [29][129].
- **Population.** Catalhoyuk revised in 2024 to 600-800 (older estimates of 2,800-10,000 assumed all houses stood at once) [111]; Jericho 200-300 to 2,000-3,000 [179]; Skara Brae perhaps 50 [27].

## 3. Bronze, with the Copper Age

- **Dates.** Chalcolithic c. 6000-3500 BC in West Asia, 6200-2200 BC in Europe, a 300-year blip in Britain; its chronology is contested [163]. Bronze Age c. 3300-1200 BC in the Near East, 3200-900 BC in Europe; a culture qualifies if it smelts copper and alloys it with tin or arsenic, or trades for bronze [35].
- **Enabling chain.** Pottery kilns reach the c. 1,100 C that copper needs; the oldest secure smelting is Belovode c. 5000 BC [163]. Tin is scarce, so bronze depends on long-distance trade: the Uluburun wreck (c. 1320 BC) carried 10 t of Cypriot copper and 1 t of tin, enough for 11 t of bronze [35][146]. Secondary products (milk, wool, traction, riding, pack transport) scale up in the 4th millennium BC, needing woolly sheep, wheel, ard and new species [134]. Ox-drawn ard on light soils, a ploughed field c. 2800 BC [135]; wagons c. 3500 BC, spoked wheels c. 2000 BC, and no practical wheel in Mesoamerica for want of draught animals [86]; sails by c. 3200 BC and Late Bronze Age seaborne trade [90]; horse domestication c. 3500 BC (disputed) or c. 2200 BC (genomics) [145].
- **Settlement.** Uruk c. 50,000 at its peak c. 3100 BC (archaeologists are more cautious): canals to the Euphrates, temple precincts with a full-time bureaucracy, mass-produced ration bowls, a 9 km wall c. 3000 BC [78]. Mohenjo-daro (c. 2500-1900 BC): baked-brick grid, covered drains with settling traps, house bathrooms, perhaps 700 wells [75]. Canals at Choga Mami c. 6000 BC [180]; over 4,000 branch canals around Eridu [40].
- **Production chains.** Irrigated barley to temple store to rations; copper plus traded tin plus charcoal to bronze; clay to brick and wheel-thrown pots; wool to cloth; flax to sailcloth [134][90][78][35].
- **Civic life and hazards.** Childe's city criteria: dense population, specialists, surplus taken as tithe, monuments, a ruling class, writing, exact sciences, long-distance trade [151]. Writing grew from accounting: tokens c. 7500 BC, impressed tablets c. 3350-3100 BC, phonetic signs c. 3000 BC, literature c. 2000 BC [67][89]; scribal schools trained a small elite [72]; nilometers set tax from the flood height [33]. The collapse c. 1200 BC broke the tin trade [35].
- **Population.** Capitals of 40,000-65,000 (Uruk, Ur; the Ur figure is unsourced); ordinary towns a few thousand [78].

## 4. Iron

- **Dates.** From c. 1300 BC in Anatolia, c. 1200 BC in the Near East, c. 800 BC in central and western Europe, c. 500 BC in Scandinavia; it ends c. 550 BC in the Near East and c. AD 800 in Scandinavia, so the label names a technology, not a span of years [83].
- **Enabling chain.** Bloomery smelting needs over 1,250 C and never melts the iron; the bloom is hammered free of slag and can be carburised and quenched into steel [171][88]. Snodgrass blames a tin shortage c. 1300 BC; once mastered, iron was cheaper, stronger and lighter than cast bronze, though the shortage lacks direct evidence and the Hittite-monopoly story is rejected [83][171]. Blooms grew from c. 2 kg to 10-15 kg under Rome; ore to charcoal about 1:1 [88]. Detachable, replaceable ploughshares c. 1000 BC in the Near East, iron shares from c. 500 BC in China [135]; isolated iron tools such as a chisel from Heegermühle go back to c. 1000 BC [171]; iron reaping-hooks, scythes, saws and hammers in every farmer's hands by La Tène D (100-15 BC) [83]. Alphabet: Proto-Sinaitic c. 1800 BC, Phoenician c. 1050 BC, Greek c. 800 BC, literacy without a scribal class [89]. Coinage: Lydian electrum c. 650-560 BC, the stamp vouching for the metal, small silver for retail c. 600-550 BC, minting linked to tax [69].
- **Settlement.** Hill forts and oppida; the polis and its agora, first a gathering place, later a market [39][58]. The findings give this era little settlement detail of its own; paved streets and water supply belong to the next.
- **Production chains.** Ore and charcoal to bloom to bar to tool [88]; silver to coin to wages and tax; grain to a retail market [69].
- **Civic life and hazards.** Coin lets a state pay and tax in money and markets run retail [69]; alphabetic literacy spreads law [89]. Famine, and raiding by larger iron-armed forces [83].
- **Population.** No typical figure in the findings; the next era's provincial town of 5,000-11,000 is the nearest benchmark [62].

## 5. Classical

- **Dates.** From Homer and the first Greek alphabetic inscriptions (8th century BC) to 476; the end is disputed between the 3rd and 8th centuries [39].
- **Enabling chain.** Gearing plus running water gives the watermill (Philo c. 280-220 BC, Vitruvius 40/10 BC); donkey mills by c. 300 BC; Barbegal's 16 wheels fed 12,500-40,000 people a day, estimates differing threefold [116]. Lime, volcanic ash and formwork give concrete, widespread from c. 150 BC, underwater at Baiae by the late 2nd century BC and at Caesarea's harbour in 22-15 BC, the Pantheon c. AD 125 [119]. Surveying, the arch and state labour give the Aqua Appia and Via Appia (312 BC), then eleven aqueducts with a combined conduit length of about 780-800 km, 47 km of it above ground [11], and a road network of 120,000-400,000 km depending on definition [3]. Aqueducts fed fountains first, then baths, then paying private users [11]; their overflow kept the sewer running, with public latrines from the 2nd century BC and house connections only c. AD 100 for the rich [48]. Glassblowing (1st century BC) made glass an everyday container and window [43]; concrete let insulae rise five storeys, capped by Augustus at about 70 feet [46].
- **Settlement.** Grid plans wherever one authority founds a town on open land: Timgad (c. AD 100), 111 blocks on a cardo and decumanus [162]. Rome's 354 catalogue lists 952 baths [24]; bread was bought from commercial bakeries [116].
- **Production chains.** Grain fleets from Africa and Egypt to warehouses to a dole of 5 modii a month for 200,000 under Augustus [98]; water to fountains, baths and sewers [11][48]; stone, lime and ash to concrete; sand and soda to glass [119][43].
- **Civic life and hazards.** Services bought with patronage: baths, sponsored games (57 fixed days of ludi in the late Republic, more than 135 days a year under the Empire) [181], free grain [24][98]. The vigiles (AD 6), funded by a 4% tax on slave sales, were the first standing fire and night watch [16]. The fire of AD 64 destroyed three of fourteen districts and brought building rules and a new street plan [10]. The Plague of Justinian (541-549) killed a fifth to 40% of Constantinople on disputed estimates, with no civic response but tax [138]. Rome fell to c. 30,000 by 600 [139].
- **Population.** Rome c. 450,000 by a recent estimate (a million traditionally); an average Roman city of 54 ha held 5,000-11,000 [62].

## 6. Medieval

- **Dates.** c. 500-1500: Early (5th-10th century, decline and counter-urbanisation), High (c. 1000-1300, growth, universities, cathedrals, a warm period), Late (1300-1500, famine, plague, war) [139].
- **Enabling chain.** The heavy mouldboard plough (recorded 643, general by the early 9th century with three fields) needed four to eight oxen, so households pooled teams and ploughed long strips; iron mouldboards from the 10th century [84]. Three fields cut fallow from a half to a third; spring oats fed horses, which with the collar (c. 8th century) and horseshoe (9th) replaced oxen [92][42]. Lynn White's thesis that this caused the boom is widely contested; one 2013 test credits the plough with over 10% of the rise in density [68][63]. Watermills: 5,624-6,082 in Domesday England, about one per 200-300 people on Darby's Domesday population of 1.2-1.6 million [182], 10,000-15,000 by 1300, driving fulling, sawing, ore, bellows and paper (1282) [116]. Post windmill 1185, horizontal treadle loom 11th century, spinning wheel 13th, clocks and spectacles from the 1280s, blast furnaces 1150-1350, cannon by 1324, compass to caravel c. 1190-1440s [92]. Gutenberg (c. 1440) recombined paper, cast type, oil ink and the screw press into 3,600 pages a day [122][65]; 236 towns had presses by 1500 and 8-20 million books were printed, estimates differing [77].
- **Settlement.** Timber-framed, jettied houses on narrow plots [31]; chimneys from the 1100s, common by the 1300s, fostering private rooms and upper floors [100]; London importing sea coal from the early 1200s, with smoke complaints by the 1280s (commission of inquiry 1288) and a royal ban on coal in lime kilns in 1307 [155]; the Great Conduit (c. 1245) piping spring water to a public cistern [79]; cesspits and gong farmers, 16 public latrines for 30,000 Londoners c. 1400 [73]; bastides on a grid around an arcaded market square, walls later [85].
- **Production chains.** Seed-to-harvest 1:3 to 1:4 on manorial accounts [120]; wool to fulling mill to cloth; timber to charcoal to furnace; rag to paper to print; coin to bills of exchange, the Medici Bank's main profit [92][149].
- **Civic life and hazards.** Communes bought charters for markets, self-government and walls [30]; 2,000 English markets chartered 1200-1349, a rival barred within a day's travel, guilds in guildhalls [58]; universities from the 11th century [139]; waqf and church hospitals from 805 [91]; Rijnland water board 1255 [61]; the Statute of Winchester (1285) set unpaid night watches until 1829 [161]. Catastrophe before institution: the Great Famine (1315-17) killed 10-15% [150]; the Black Death a third to a half, raising wages that the Statute of Labourers (1351) tried to freeze [112]; quarantine at Ragusa (1377) and Venice's lazaretto (1423) within two generations [168].
- **Population.** Europe from c. 56 million to 73.5-100 million, 1000-1300 [183]; London 80,000-100,000 c. 1300, most English towns 1,000-5,000, Venice and Florence c. 100,000 [176].

## 7. Renaissance and Early Modern

- **Dates.** No agreed bounds: printing (1440s), 1453, 1492 or the Reformation, to the Industrial Revolution or 1776-1815 [80].
- **Enabling chain.** Printing, universities, lenses and patronage give the Scientific Revolution (1543-1687), institutionalised by the Royal Society (1660) and the French Academy (1666) [148]. Cranked pumps (1405), De re metallica (1556) and the dry dock (1495) deepen mines and enlarge ships [65]. Compass, rudder, astrolabe and caravel give ocean voyages, the Columbian exchange and mercantilism [92][80]. The four-course rotation removes fallow before 1700 [125]; with enclosure, the Rotherham plough (1730), breeding and drainage, output per labourer rose 2.5x (1700-1850) and one farm household fed five instead of 1.2 [5]. Paper money: Song China 1024, Stockholms Banco 1661, each followed by over-issue [123].
- **Settlement.** Paris lanterns from 1588, maintained street lights from 1667 [103]; the New River (1613) brought spring water into London, later distributed to customers' houses by the New River Company; the London Bridge Waterworks switched its mains on a weekly rota [142]; Harington's closet (1596) failed without mains and sewers until Cummings' S-trap (1775) [153]; the 1667 Rebuilding Act required brick, banned jetties and fixed house heights by street class [105]; Pombal's gaiola made seismic resistance a code after 1755 [141].
- **Production chains.** Rags to paper to books [122]; colonial coffee and sugar to coffeehouses [140]; fodder to wintered stock to manure to yield [125]; private capital and royal charter to a water company [142].
- **Civic life and hazards.** The Theatre (1576), a penny to stand [137]; the parish poor rate (1601) [53]; coffeehouses (1650s) carrying news and breeding Lloyd's and the stock exchange [140]; the Great Plague (1665) killing a quarter of London under 40-day quarantine and shut-up houses [143]; compulsory schooling in Prussia (1763) [94]. Towns were population sinks until c. 1770, growing only by immigration [114].
- **Population.** London c. 400,000 in 1665 [143]; England 5.5 million in 1700 [5].

## 8. Industrial

- **Dates.** First Industrial Revolution c. 1760-1840 (debated), second c. 1870-1914 [8][38]. Allen: Britain combined high wages with cheap coal, so replacing labour with fuel paid there first [66].
- **Enabling chain.** Coke smelting (1709) went mostly to castings until the 1750s; puddling (1784) gave cheap wrought iron [8]. Newcomen's engine (1712) drained mines where coal was free; Watt's condenser (1765) waited nine years for a bored cylinder (1774) and used 75% less coal; by 1800 about 2,000 engines were at work in Britain, 496 of them built by Boulton and Watt between 1775 and 1800 [184][185]. The flying shuttle (1733) starved looms of yarn; the jenny (1764), water frame (1769) and mule (1779) then outran looms until the power loom spread after 1805 [8]. The Bridgewater Canal (1761) halved Manchester's coal price [102]; macadam from 1816; the Liverpool and Manchester Railway (1830) ran steam to a timetable [8]; gas light from 1807, a gasworks in every large town within 15 years [19]. Second revolution: Bessemer steel (1856) cut prices about six-fold and rails lasted ten times longer [23]; Pearl Street (1882) lit 85 customers with 400 lamps, Niagara AC 1895, 70% of US homes electrified by 1930 [12]; Drake's well 1859, Benz 1886 [38]; Haber-Bosch ammonia 1913 [44]. Factory electrification passed 50% only in the 1920s, after plants were rebuilt around a motor per machine [128].
- **Settlement.** Back-to-back terraces from the 1790s [110]; Manchester 70,409 (1801) to 303,382 (1851) [7]; the omnibus (1829) and horsecar (1832) stretched the two-mile walking city to four [60]; Sprague's trolley (1888) [113] gave streetcar suburbs within a ten-minute walk of the line [54]; water steam-pumped, slow-sand filtered from 1829 and by law from 1852, on constant supply only around 1900 [142].
- **Production chains.** Coal to coke to iron to rails; coal to gas to light; cotton to yarn to cloth in factories; refrigerated ships (1876-82) landing 760,000 t of meat a year by 1910 [18].
- **Civic life and hazards.** Edinburgh's 1824 fire gave the first municipal brigade and Tooley Street (1861) the public Metropolitan Fire Brigade [147]; Peel's police (1829) [144]; cholera (1848) brought the Public Health Act [76]; the Great Stink (1858) pushed the sewer bill through Parliament between 15 June and 2 August, and cholera's last London outbreak (1866) struck only the East End not yet connected to the new sewers [172]; germ theory beat miasma by the 1880s [87]; vaccination free 1840, compulsory 1853 [71]; Birkenhead Park 1847, Libraries Act 1850 [32][81]; board schools 1870, compulsory 1880, free 1891 [70]; Bismarck's insurance 1883-89 [104].
- **Population.** England 8.3 million (1801) to 31 million (1901); Manchester 10,000 (1717) to 2.3 million (1911); 3% urban in 1800, half by 2007 (UN) [8][186].

## 9. Modern

- **Dates.** c. 1900 onward; the Information Age from mid-century; a fourth industrial revolution is debated [133].
- **Enabling chain.** Interchangeable parts, conveyors, motors and cheap steel give Ford's moving line (1913): 12.5 man-hours to 93 minutes, $825 (1909) to $260 (1925), 15 million built; the car came before paved roads and rural power [187]. Cars destroyed macadam, so tarmac (1902) followed [159]. Light engines gave the Wright Flyer (1903) [136]; valves and household power gave radio (1920-22, most US homes by 1931) and television (most by 1955) [158]; deep-tank fermentation made penicillin a mass drug by 1945 [14]; Chicago Pile-1 (1942) led to Calder Hall (1956) and c. 300 GW by 1990, each accident slowing construction [52]; fertiliser plus dwarf wheat and rice doubled developing-world cereals 1961-85 [118]; transistor 1947, integrated circuit 1958-59, microprocessor 1971 [133]; ARPANET 1969, Web 1991, iPhone 2007, majority smartphone ownership in the US 2013 (Pew, 56% of adults) [156][188]; solar modules down over 99% since the mid-1970s [50].
- **Settlement.** 1916 zoning separated uses and stepped the skyscraper [21]; Levittown (1947-51) built 30 houses a day on FHA loans with no transit [126]; tower blocks on lifts and prefabricated concrete until Ronan Point (1968) [55]; chlorinated water from 1908 and typhoid collapsed [37]; the nickelodeon (1905) reached 26 million a week by 1910 [45].
- **Production chains.** Oil to petrol and plastics; electricity to motors, lifts and media; ammonia to grain; silicon to chips, networks and solar [44][133][50].
- **Civic life and hazards.** Infectious deaths fell from 11 to under 1 per 1,000, mostly from food, water and hygiene before mid-century medicine; births fell later [109]. Kanto 1923 killed 105,000, 87% by fire, and a December law took land for firebreak streets; California mandated reinforced masonry in 1933 [154]. The 1953 North Sea surge produced the Delta Works to a 1-in-10,000-year standard and the Thames Barrier (1984) [97]. Adoption lags average 45 years, shrinking from 50 before 1950 to 15 after 2000 [132].
- **Population.** Half of humanity urban by 2007 (UN) [186].

**Lessons, stated as observations**

1. Three families exist: gate ages where one purchase flips the roster (Age of Empires, Empire Earth), ladder eras where an era is a slice of a tree and old buildings persist (Forge of Empires, Rise of Cultures), and needs ladders with no era where each house climbs as one more good reaches it (Caesar, Anno, Manor Lords); Dawn of Man is a hybrid [173][34][157][51][20].
2. Era count tracks session length: four for an hour, six to eight for ten to twenty hours, twenty for years of check-ins [173][82][13][20].
3. What reviewers remember is the visible flip: colour grading and music, paved roads and stone walls, house art [107][131][64].
4. A prerequisite the player builds reads better than one that is only paid [173][1][124].
5. A choice at the moment of advance is praised across the Age of Empires family [28][167][1].
6. Repeating the same loop with bigger numbers is the main complaint against long ladders, and 50-year eras show the cost of over-slicing [93][57].
7. Research that runs out while the city continues leaves a rut [127][47][115].
8. Hard resets at transitions read as loss [9].
9. Designers have converged on advancement by doing (Eurekas, era stars, milestones, population tiers); critics of fixed trees say every path becomes "out-research the rival" and veterans memorise a golden path, which Old World breaks with a random four-card draw [117][164][106][25][152].
10. Later-age starts are undocumented in the sources; ladder games forbid them and their early eras read as a tutorial tax [34][57].

## Three ways to cut the ages for Townlet

Each list names the ages, then what the cut gains and loses against the evidence above. None is recommended.

### Five ages

1. Forager (Palaeolithic and Mesolithic)
2. Village (Neolithic and Copper)
3. City (Bronze, Iron and Classical)
4. Town (Medieval and Early Modern)
5. Machine city (Industrial and Modern)

Gains: each age is a different loop (gather; farm; irrigate, trade and administer; mill and market; fuel and grid), close to Age of Empires' four-plus-one and a single session [173]. Each age has a clear energy step: muscle, animals, water, wind and coal, oil and grid. Loses: Bronze's tin-trade dependency and the Classical engineered city merge, so the "imported resource" gate and the aqueduct-to-sewer chain land in one age [35][11]; medieval contraction and the print-and-science era vanish; the largest visual change in the record, coal city to car city, is one step [8][123]; every age must carry two or three building sets.

### Seven ages

1. Forager
2. Village (Neolithic)
3. Bronze city
4. Classical city (Iron and Classical)
5. Medieval town
6. Coal town (Early Modern and first Industrial)
7. Motor city (second Industrial and Modern)

Gains: the trade-dependent Bronze age stands alone, as does the medieval mill-and-market town; roughly one energy source per age; seven is close to Dawn of Man's six and Rise of Nations' eight, the nearest analogues for a long single-player arc [59][20]. Loses: folding Iron into Classical hides the bloomery-to-coin step, though the findings give that era little settlement content of its own [83][69]; print and science are folded into the coal age; the last age carries both the 1880s grid and the 2010s phone [12][156].

### Nine ages

Palaeolithic and Mesolithic; Neolithic; Bronze; Iron; Classical; Medieval; Renaissance and Early Modern; Industrial; Modern, as in the table above.

Gains: matches the reference periodisation one to one, so every era section and source above maps to an age; a visible flip every age; the infrastructure chain (well, conduit, aqueduct, mains, steam pump, filter, sewer) spreads one step per age [75][11][79][142][172]. Loses: Iron on its own is thin for a city builder; nine gates in a session means short ages or a long session; Rise of Cultures shows that over-slicing makes adjacent ages look alike [57]; nine sets of building art, palettes and roads.

## Open questions

- Which family of progression: gate, ladder, needs ladder, or a hybrid like Dawn of Man's milestones plus one era tech?
- What is the age-up currency: stockpiled surplus plus built institutions (Age of Empires II), milestones (Dawn of Man, Humankind), or population by class (Anno)?
- The island has no tin. Does Bronze force an off-island trade partner, and does that become a general mechanic for later imports (coal, oil)?
- Starting in a later age: does the earlier ages' infrastructure already exist, or must it be built? The record shows things lost and reinvented (Indus drains, Roman sewers, central heating absent in Britain c. 400-1900) [48][62].
- Should the first disaster of each type unlock its mitigation (fire then brick, cholera then sewers, flood then water board), as the record repeatedly shows [61][105][172]?
- Do old buildings persist across ages (Forge of Empires) or obsolesce (Empire Earth)?
- Should pre-1800 towns need immigration to grow, given the urban graveyard [114]?
- Does energy (wood, charcoal, water, wind, coal, oil, grid) become the spine of the tree, with food, materials, transport and information as lanes?
- What session length is the target, since that sets the age count?
- How much does each age change the look: palette, building art, road surface, lighting, vehicles?
- Is the pottery-before-farming or farming-before-pottery order fixed, or can the same unlocks be reached in more than one order [2]?
- Where does the current Townlet (zones, services, cars) sit, and does its economy become the last age or the last two?
- Should the game adopt later-era pacing, since adoption lags shrink from 50 years to 15 [132]?
- The three-age scheme is a Europe and Near East construct [56]; does the generic Old World framing need any non-European content at all?
